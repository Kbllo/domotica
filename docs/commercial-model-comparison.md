# Commercial model comparison

Date: 2026-09-20. Status: advisory comparison requested by the founder; no business model, spending, quotation, or contract approved.

## Context and definitions

The founder wants low initial investment, controlled risk, and worthwhile returns, with a personal focus on design and configuration. The preferred first route to explore is a builder purchasing for new homes. Both new and occupied homes remain in scope for the business. Capital limits, supplier terms, project quantities, actual workload, and willingness to pay are unknown.

- **Services only:** the founder specifies and configures the system. The builder buys equipment directly from a supplier and receives that supplier's equipment invoice. Selection, purchasing coordination, inspection, and warranty assistance are paid services only to the extent included in the agreed scope.
- **Services and equipment:** the business buys and resells specified equipment and provides the agreed design/configuration services. It manages procurement and the obligations associated with its role as seller. This does not mean the founder personally performs electrical installation.

Physical installation must have a separately identified responsible party and payer in either model. Neither model is an equity partnership with the builder. Domestic sourcing is the comparison baseline; importing is an additional decision requiring its own economics and compliance review.

## Benefits, risks, and practical controls

| Dimension | Services only | Services and equipment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capital and cash | Equipment does not have to pass through the founder's balance sheet or be financed by the founder. Service labor, travel, tools, delayed collections, and rework still require funding. | Purchases may precede collection. Suitable advance payments can reduce the funding gap but do not remove delivery, refund, or warranty exposure. |
| Sources of contribution | Fees for design, configuration, agreed inspections, handover, and explicitly contracted assistance. Profitability depends on charging for all work and limiting revisions. | The same service fees plus any equipment contribution remaining after procurement, handling, shipping, payment costs, extra labor, and expected after-sales costs. More revenue does not establish higher net profit. |
| Purchasing control | The builder may substitute models, buy incompatible variants, purchase too late, or lose invoices. Specify exact requirements and review substitutions before purchase; agree what additional work changes generate. | More control over models, suppliers, consistency, receiving checks, and replacement stock. That control entails workload, supplier dependency, damaged goods, and return management. |
| Builder experience | The builder coordinates at least the equipment supplier and the service provider. A paid coordination scope can simplify this while preserving direct purchase. | The builder has one commercial supplier for the contracted equipment and services. This can be attractive, but may concentrate all complaints on the business even when another trade caused the issue. |
| Warranty and problem diagnosis | A direct hardware purchase gives the builder a claim relationship with its seller. It does not remove the founder's responsibility for design, configuration, or services actually promised. Diagnostic visits can consume unpriced time. | Equipment claims and replacement logistics become part of the seller's exposure. Supplier remedies must be checked for coverage of shipping, removal, reinstallation, configuration, and delay; do not assume they cover every downstream cost. |
| Scheduling | Delivery depends partly on the builder's purchasing discipline. Confirm materials and installation readiness before scheduling configuration, and agree how additional visits are handled. | The business can align procurement with deployment, but bears the consequences of supplier delays and stocking decisions within its commitments. |
| Commercial positioning | Requires explaining the value of expertise and handover. A builder may compare visible equipment prices, undervalue design, or use the specification to contract someone else. Charge for substantive design and define reusable versus project-specific deliverables. | Can package an outcome and negotiate purchasing terms. Online price comparisons and demands for discounts can still compress the hardware margin. Service fees should remain economically justified on their own. |
| Growth | Can grow through repeatable designs and configuration without financing every device, but still depends on founder hours and trained delivery capacity. | May benefit from standardized purchasing and supplier relationships, but cash exposure and after-sales obligations grow with unit count and open projects. |

In both models, distinguish the builder who buys from the resident who uses the system. Define readiness for configuration, acceptance, account ownership transfer, warranty contacts, initial training, and any later service. One sale for multiple homes does not mean one home's worth of delivery or support.

## Management economics

Compare identical delivered functions and explicit responsibility assignments. Record prices excluding IVA for economic comparisons; cash forecasts must include actual tax payment timing and any recovery that is legally available. Do not select a tax regime or assume tax recovery from this document.

`service_contribution = service_revenue - all_variable_service_costs`

`equipment_incremental_contribution = equipment_revenue - purchase_cost - incremental_procurement_and_delivery_costs - incremental_labor - expected_incremental_after_sales_costs`

`combined_contribution = service_contribution + equipment_incremental_contribution`

Classify costs once. Founder labor must appear even when not paid out immediately. Contribution precedes fixed costs, financing, and income tax, and is not freely distributable cash. Customer advances must retain sufficient backing for delivery and any applicable resolution obligations.

### Arithmetic illustration, not a quote or forecast

All figures below are invented MXN amounts excluding IVA. They demonstrate the effect of extra work; they are not Puebla prices, supplier quotations, recommended margins, or measured probabilities. Physical installation is assumed to be paid separately by the builder under the same arrangement in both columns.

| Item | Services only | Services and equipment |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Service revenue | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Variable service costs, including founder labor, travel, and normal support allowance | 6,000 | 6,000 |
| Equipment resale revenue received by this business | 0 | 25,000 |
| Equipment purchase cost paid by this business | 0 | 20,000 |
| Additional procurement, logistics, founder labor, and expected hardware after-sales costs, beyond the common service costs | 0 | 2,000 |
| Total contribution before fixed costs, financing, and income tax | 4,000 | 7,000 |

The services-only builder still purchases equipment separately; the zero entries describe this business's revenue and expenses, not the customer's full project cost. The equipment selling-price/purchase-price spread is 5,000, while its incremental contribution is only 3,000 after the additional costs.

Sensitivity, holding the common service scope constant:

- **Lower additional workload:** if additional equipment-related costs are 1,000 instead of 2,000, combined contribution is 8,000.
- **Illustrative base:** additional costs of 2,000 produce combined contribution of 7,000.
- **Adverse event:** an additional unrecovered cost of 3,000 beyond the base allowances reduces combined contribution to 4,000, equal to the service-only illustration, with extra seller obligations already assumed.

No ROI or payback is calculated: capital-at-risk and cash timing are unknown. An order funded by an advance can have a small initial funding gap while still carrying substantial obligations. Neither model has been demonstrated profitable from actual project data.

## Provisional recommendation for the first project

Start the discussion with **paid design and configuration, with builder-direct equipment purchases from a specified list**. Include purchasing coordination or receiving checks as clearly priced services if the builder wants them. This recommendation follows the founder's stated preference for low investment and technical design/configuration, and the current absence of validated supplier margins, workload, and loss limits. It is not a final model selection.

Before undertaking substantive project design, agree a paid deliverable, required inputs, revisions, and payment milestones. Preserve normal contractual responsibility for errors in the founder's own work. Agree how substitutions, incomplete electrical work, rescheduling, and new requests affect scope and fees.

Reconsider equipment resale for a defined project when all of the following are supported:

1. The builder values a single supplier and accepts a price that compensates for the additional work and exposure.
2. Exact models, current supplier quotations, delivery dates, documentation, return terms, and replacement processes are confirmed.
3. Agreed payment timing funds procurement without exceeding the founder's still-to-be-defined cash and loss limits. Do not fund equipment on the assumption that the builder will pay only after selling the home.
4. Additional contribution remains worthwhile after realistic purchasing labor, logistics, expected failures, and an adverse scenario. The service component is separately costed.
5. Order size, cancellation exposure, stock held, and after-sales capacity are explicitly bounded. Do not purchase speculative inventory merely to obtain an apparent unit discount.

For either route, measure actual hours by activity, unplanned visits, changes, collections, and support for a bounded first scope. Use those results to decide whether equipment contribution justifies resale on subsequent units. Stop dependent procurement if payment, specification, or delivery readiness differs materially from the agreement; do not silently absorb a new financing commitment.

## Legal scope to validate before contracting

Mexico's LFPC defines final consumers and limited circumstances involving purchases incorporated into third-party production or commercialization (Article 2). It also sets rules for offered warranties and their fulfillment, including distributor involvement (Articles 77–79). A builder buying for resale and an eventual resident must not automatically be treated as the same legal relationship. Source consulted on 2026-09-20: [Cámara de Diputados — LFPC](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LFPC.pdf).

Before issuing contractual terms, have the actual transaction chain and applicable responsibilities reviewed. A phrase such as "manufacturer warranty only" does not, by itself, establish that all seller obligations have been transferred. This comparison does not determine the project's legal classification, allocate all liability, or set warranty durations. No contract has been drafted or approved.

## Inputs required for an actual choice

The founder's deployable funds, acceptable loss and weekly time; the builder's payment and purchasing preferences; the project's bill of materials and quantities; supplier terms; and the actual service workload. These inputs can change the recommendation. No contact, purchasing, import, or live-home action is authorized by this document.
